25                                          Abstract

26

27            Similarity notions between vertices in a graph, such as structural and regular equiv-

28            alence, are one of the main ingredients in clustering tools in complex network science.

29            We generalise structural and regular equivalences for undirected hypergraphs and pro-

30            vide a characterisation of structural and regular equivalences of undirected graphs and

31            hypergraphs through neighbourhood graphs and Olivier-Ricci curvature. Our charac-

32            terisation sheds new light on these similarity notions opening a new avenue for their

33            exploration. These characterisations also enable the construction of a possibly wide
34
              family of regular partitions, thereby offering a new route to a task that has so far been
35

36            computationally challenging.

37

38            Keywords: Ollivier-Ricci curvature, regular equivalence, structural equivalence, positional

39            analysis, random walks, neighbourhood graphs, hypergraphs

14  1 Introduction

15

16  Social Network Analysis is a branch of social science characterised by a structuralist approach
17

18  that puts the focus on studying relationships between social actors rather than the actors

19  themselves. The field has been developed since at least the 1930s, see [Fre04] for a historical

20  overview, and [WF94; Pat93] for an overview of the main methods and questions, and [BE05;

21  Raw+23] for a more modern account of some of the methods. One of the main questions in
22
    the field is how to define a position occupied by a given social actor in a system. Different
23

24  equivalence relations have been introduced to capture what it means for two actors to occupy

25  similar social positions. The first of these was the notion of structural equivalence: two actors

26  are structurally equivalent if they interact in the same way with the same actors. This notion

27  is too rigid to be useful in practice, and thus one relaxes it through more flexible notions:
28
    on one hand one is interested in studying measures of approximate structural equivalence,
29

30  capturing the extent to which two actors are structurally equivalent, and on the other hand

31  one looks for more flexible equivalence relations, of which the most flexible one is given by

32  regular equivalence. Two actors are regularly equivalent if they interact with actors who are

33  themselves equivalent.
34
    Regular equivalence is a more flexible notion than structural equivalence, however more
35

36  difficult to compute in practice. The set of partitions satisfying the properties of a regular

37  equivalence forms a lattice, and several algorithms for computing some elements of this

38  lattice have been proposed, such as REGE [BE93], an algorithm that iteratively refines

39  partitions and returns the maximal regular equivalence. More recently, algorithms that
40
    compute approximate regular partitions have been proposed in [Squ+25]. An overview on
41

42  different algorithms and challenges can be found in [EB19]. The situation becomes even

43  more challenging in hypergraphs, which model interactions between two or more actors.

44  While recent years have seen advances in the development of generalisations of methods

45  from graphs to hypergraphs [Bic+23; ZHS07; Tor+21], the development of structural and
46
    regular equivalence notions and algorithms for hypergraphs are essentially absent.
47

48  In our work we propose a geometric characterisation of structural equivalence based on

49  Ollivier­Ricci curvature. Ollivier-Ricci curvature [Oll09] is a generalised notion of Ricci

50  curvature for metric measure spaces, defined by comparing probability measures on neigh-

51  bourhoods via the optimal transportation distance. Intuitively, curvature along the path
52
    connecting two vertices is positive if their neighbourhoods can be mapped to each other
53

54  with little transportation cost. This makes curvature a natural tool to capture neighbour-

55  hood overlap in structural equivalence, and allows us to define different types of measures

56

57

58

60

9             knowledge, the first algorithm to compute some regular partitions for hypergrahs.

30            1.1 Related work

31

32            To the best of our knowledge, connections between curvature and structural or regular

33            equivalences have not been studied before. A major part of our work consists in relating

34            the connected component structure of neighborhood graphs based on paths with regular or
35
              structural classes. While neighborhood graphs based on paths have been studied since at
36

37            least the 1980s, see [Sim83; BGK19], they are less well known than neighborhood graphs

38            based on walks. The latter have been extensively studied in complex network science and

39            adjacent fields because of their relationship with powers of the adjacency matrix of a graph.

40            In particular, their connection with Ollivier Ricci curvature has been studied in [Frank].
41
              Until 2025, generalisations of regular equivalences for hypergraphs have only been consid-
42

43            ered, to the best of our knowledge, in the PhD thesis by Borgatti [Bor89], see also the review

44            article [EB93], in which Borgatti developed an equivalent notion of regular equivalence, based

45            on regular colorings.

46            A framework relying on the theory of coalgebras that extends regular equivalences as
47
              well as the associated positional and role analyses --i.e., respectively, the study of quotient
48

49            graphs in which nodes are given by regular classes, and the associated semigroups generated

50            by adjacency matrices and their quotients-- from graphs to hypergraphs has recently been

51            developed in work by one of the authors [MOR26].

52            To the best of our knowledge, algorithms to compute regular partitions based on neigh-
53
              bourhood graphs and curvature have not been considered before in the literature. Our
54

55            approach is thus fundamentally different from existing approaches that compute iterative

56            refinements of partitions [BE93; Squ+25].

35  2.1 Equivalence relations on graphs

36

37  Definition 2.3. Given an undirected graph G, and an equivalence relation  on its set of

38  vertices, we say that the equivalence relation is a:

39

41  {a, c} is an edge in G if and only if {b, c} is an edge in G.
42

43  · Regular equivalence if for all vertices a, b we have that a  b if and only if:
44
    if {a, c} is an edge in G then there exists d such that {b, d} is an edge and c  d.
45

46

47  Given a partition of the set of vertices of a graph G, we say that it is a structural,

48  respectively regular partition partition if it satisfies the properties of a structural, resp.

49  regular equivalence relation. Similarly, we call the blocks of such a partition structural
50
    resp. regular classes of G.
51

53

54  one is the partition with only singleton blocks. Every structural equivalence is a regular

55  equivalence. On the other hand, not every regular equivalence is a structural equivalence,

56  and in fact, there are in general many different partitions on the set of vertices of a given

12  3.1 Connected components of n-neighbourhood graphs
13

14  As n increases, the n-neighbourhood graph Gn can become disconnected very quickly. For
15
    instance, once n exceeds the diameter of the graph, this phenomenon is easily observed in
16

17  the case of a star graph with m edges: G2 already has two connected components, while G3

18  consists of m + 1 isolated vertices. Here we first provide an upper bound on the number of

19  connected components of G2.
20

21  Lemma 3.1. Let G be a connected graph. Then G2 has at most two connected components.
22  More precisely:
23

24  1. If G is non-bipartite (i.e., it contains an odd cycle), then G2 is connected.

25

26  2. If G is bipartite with partition of the set of vertices V = V0  V1, then G2 has exactly

27

28  two connected components with vertex sets V0 and V1.

29

30  Proof. We note that if G is bipartite with partition of the vertex set given by V0 and V1, then

31  by definition there are no edges between nodes belonging to V0 nor between nodes belonging

32  to V1. Since G is connected, there exists a path between any two vertices in V0, and likewise
33  between any two vertices in V1. Such paths necessarily alternate between the two partitions
34
    at each step and hence have even length. Conversely, paths between a given node in V0 and
35

36  a given node in V1 must have odd length. Consequently, V0 and V1 are the vertex sets of the

37  two connected components of G2, and therefore G2 has exactly two components.

38  If G is not bipartite, we show that G2 is connected, i.e., that any two vertices are con-

39

40  nected by a path in G2. Let x and y be arbitrary vertices of G. Since G is connected, there

41  exists at least one path between them. If such a path has even length, then x and y lie in

42  the same connected component of G2.
43

44

45  be a path of odd length between them; write this path as

3   Remark 4.12 (Hierarchy of structural equivalence notions in hypergraphs). The proof of

4 Accepted ManuscriptLemma 4.11 relies on strong local assumptions, most notably the uniqueness of hyperedges
5
    connecting a vertex to each of its neighbours, as well as equality of degrees.
6

7       These assumptions are sufficient to ensure that equality of the EE measures µx and µy

8   forces a complete matching of local hyperedge cardinalities, but they are not intrinsic to

9   the definition of the EE random walk or to EE-ORC. This motivates the introduction of a

10  hierarchy of structural equivalence notions of increasing strength, reflecting different levels of
11
    combinatorial information that can be recovered from µx and from curvature-based quantities.
12

13  We note in all the following notions of equivalence we have that the neighbours of x and y are

14  identical, which means that all these notions are stronger than weak structural equivalence.

15

18                                        µx = µy.

21  condition EE-ORC(x, y) = 1. It captures equivalence with respect to one-step EE diffusion,

22  but does not encode the underlying hyperedge incidence structure uniquely.

23

24  (ii) Weighted-neighbourhood equivalence. Vertices x and y are said to be weighted-

25  neighbourhood equivalent if, for every vertex z,

34  sumption, when dx = dy, this notion coincides with EE-measure equivalence by definition of

3   5 Approximate structural equivalences for graphs and

4 Accepted Manuscript

5   hypergraphs

6

7   As we saw, structural equivalence is a very restrictive notion. Thus, in practice, one works

8   with measures of approximate structural equivalence, such as cosine similarity, see Section
9
    2.5. A different measure of approximate structural equivalence is suggested by our geometric
10

11  characterisation, not only for graphs but also for hypergraphs: we know that two vertices

12  x, y of a graph are structurally equivalent if and only if the Ollivier-Ricci curvature of (x, y)

13  is equal to 1. This result extends directly to hypergraphs: weak structural equivalence can

14  be characterised via EN-ORC, and EE-measure equivalence via EE-ORC (see Theorem 4.9
15
    and Remark 4.12). By relaxing these equalities, we obtain natural measures for approx-
16

17  imating structural equivalence: the closer the corresponding curvature is to 1, the more

18  structurally equivalent the two vertices are. In this section, we aim to better understand the

19  type of information that Ollivier­Ricci curvature provides for both undirected graphs and

20  hypergraphs.
21

22  We first make some observations.

23

24  · For vertices in a graph, the curvature (x, y) is equal to 1 if and only if x, y are

25  structurally equivalent. We note that in this case dSP(x, y) = 1. Similarly, for vertices
26  in hypergraphs, this holds with EN-ORC for weak structural equivalence and with
27
    EE-ORC for EE-measure equivalence (see Remark 4.12 for the latter).
28

29  · In both graphs and hypergraphs, if dSP(x, y) > 2, then x and y have no neighbours in

30

31  common and we call them fully dissimilar.

32

33  · In a graph, if dSP(x, y) = 2 : -1 < (x, y)  1. Therefore the closer the curvature of

34  this pair is to one, the more structurally similar they are and the closer the curvature

35  is to -1, they are less and less similar. The same statement holds for vertices x and y

36  in a hypergraph, with weak (respectively EE-measure) equivalence based on EN-ORC
37
    (respectively EE-ORC).
38

39  · If dSP(x, y) = 1 : -2 < (x, y) < 1; similar to the previous case, the closer the

40

41  curvature is to one (in this case it can never be one), the more similar the two vertices

42  are but in opposite the closer this number is to -2 , they are less and less similar.

43  Replacing (x, y) by EN-ORC or EE-ORC yields analogous conclusions for weak and
44
    EE-measure equivalences in hypergraphs, respectively.
45

46

47  Thus, for vertices at distance at most 2, the curvature is a number between -2 and 1. On

48  the other hand, cosine similarity is a number between 0 and 1. We note that cosine similarity

49  has been introduced for graphs, and a direct generalisation to hypergraphs would in general

50  provide a different type of measure, since for hypergraphs the degree of a vertex is in general
51
    smaller than the number of its neighbouring vertices. For hypergraphs, we believe that what
52

53  is of most interest is considering directly measures of approximate equivalences given by the

54  random walk equivalence notions discussed in Remark 4.12. We leave the exploration of

55  these random walk equivalence notions for future work.

49  6 Conclusion and open questions

50

51  In this work we have linked Ollivier Ricci curvature and random walks on graphs and hyper-
52
    graphs with the notions of structural and regular equivalence. We believe that our work is
53

54  only a first step in this direction, and that the connection established raises many interesting

55  questions. One of these concerns random walks on hypergraphs. Our study of characterisa-

56  tions of structural equivalences for hypergraphs through curvature highlighted the fact that

57

58

59                                                         24

60
Page 25 of 27              AUTHOR SUBMITTED MANUSCRIPT - JPCOMPX-100819.R1

1

2

3              current existing random walk notions on hypergraphs are not able to discern local adjacency

4 Accepted Manuscriptpatterns in hypergraphs; for instance, all existing notions of random walks would assign
5
               the same values to the nodes x and y in Figure 8. We can envision applications in which
6

7              this distinction might be of importance, for instance in social systems modelling, where a

8              hyperedge might represent coattendance of events, and the random walk could encode the

9              probability that a person might talk with another person during the same event. In such a

10             scenario, the situations of the two nodes x and y are drastically different, and thus should
11
               be better captured by a different type of random walk.
12

13             In extending the curvature characterisation to hypergraphs, we have noted that different

14             types of equivalences based on random walks are better suited at capturing different local

15             incidence patterns of similarity. We reserve the systematic study of these novel notions of

16             equivalences for hypergraphs to future work.
17
               Finally, we note that regular equivalences and Ollivier-Ricci curvature have recently found
18

19             application in machine learning; regular equivalences have been used in representation learn-

20             ing [Tu+18], while Ollivier-Ricci curvature has found application in the study of bottlenecks

21             and oversquashing in graph neural networks [Tah+25]. This thus raises the question of what

22             new insights the connection given in this work may give for problems in geometric deep
23

24             learning.
